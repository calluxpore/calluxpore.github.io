When I was a young boy, visiting my father’s elder brother in another city was a rare delight. His house was always filled with laughter, generous meals, and the restless energy of my cousins.

![Fear covs art1](/_assets/images/fear-covs-art1.png)

One quiet afternoon during a visit, I found myself alone in a bedroom.

I had never cared much about styling my hair. Combing it seemed unnecessary, almost a waste of time. But on the dresser, I noticed a bottle of coconut oil beside a hairbrush. Out of curiosity, I sat in front of the mirror, applied the oil, and brushed my hair thoroughly for the first time in years.

The next morning, my uncle passed away peacefully in his sleep.

I was stunned.

My young mind could not understand how someone who had been so alive the previous day could suddenly be gone. Faced with a loss too large to comprehend, my brain searched for an explanation.

It found the hairbrush.

Because I had broken my routine the day before, I began to believe that oiling and brushing my hair had somehow caused my uncle’s death.

Decades later, I understand that the connection is irrational. One event had nothing to do with the other. Yet the fear of losing someone I love became so tightly attached to that ordinary ritual that, even now, I hesitate to oil or brush my hair.

The belief disappeared.

The alarm remained.

Fear had built a home in my memory, completely detached from logic.

## Fear of the Unknown, Fear of the Remembered

Some fears emerge when we encounter what is unfamiliar. We become cautious around new environments, technologies, people, or possibilities because we cannot predict what will happen.

Other fears are built from what has already happened.

A familiar smell, object, gesture, or place can become charged with the emotional force of an earlier loss. The danger may no longer exist, but the mind continues to respond as though the past is waiting to repeat itself.

My fear of the hairbrush was not simply a fear of the object itself. It was a form of magical thinking: the belief that one private action could somehow influence an unrelated event.

It was also an attempt to create control.

A child cannot reverse death, but he can avoid a hairbrush. By turning an incomprehensible tragedy into a personal rule, the mind creates the illusion that future loss might be prevented.

Never repeat the ritual, and perhaps the tragedy will never repeat either.

Fear often survives not because it is logical, but because it offers a bargain: obey me, and I will keep you safe.

## How Ordinary Details Become Alarms

Fear does not begin in a single location in the brain. It emerges through a network of systems that evaluate danger, bodily responses, memory, and context.

The amygdala helps assign emotional significance to possible threats. The hippocampus helps encode the surrounding context: where something happened, when it happened, and what else was present.

During an intense shock, these systems can bind emotion to incidental details.

A sound.

A room.

A smell.

A bottle of coconut oil.

A hairbrush resting beside a mirror.

I think of this as _Perceptual Anchoring_: the mind fastening an overwhelming emotion to an ordinary detail that happened to be nearby.

The term is my own, but the process resembles established forms of associative learning. When two experiences occur close together, the brain may connect them even when no meaningful causal relationship exists.

The emotional event becomes the storm.

The ordinary detail becomes the lightning rod.

Once that association forms, encountering the detail again may reactivate the original alarm.

## How Memory Learns Terror

One of the most famous demonstrations of learned fear is the 1920 experiment commonly known as the Little Albert study.

An infant referred to as Albert was initially shown a white rat and displayed no obvious fear. Later, researchers presented the rat while producing a sudden, frightening metallic sound behind him.

After repeated pairings, Albert began to cry at the sight of the rat even when the noise was absent. His fear also appeared to spread to some other furry or white objects.

The experiment would be considered deeply unethical today, and aspects of its methodology and interpretation remain disputed. Yet it continues to illustrate a disturbing principle: fear can attach itself to something that was once harmless.

The rat did not become dangerous.

Albert’s experience of the rat changed.

Human memory does not preserve the past as a perfectly neutral recording. It reconstructs events through emotion, attention, expectation, and later experience.

Under certain conditions, recalling a memory can also make it temporarily malleable. This process is often called reconsolidation.

What happens during that period matters.

If a memory is recalled while the person experiences renewed anxiety, the fear may be reinforced. But if it is revisited in safety, with new information and without the expected catastrophe, its emotional meaning may gradually change.

This is why irrational fears can persist for decades.

It is also why they are not necessarily permanent.

The same mind that learned the alarm may, slowly, learn that the alarm is false.

## Do Machines Ever Panic?

The persistence of human fear raises an intriguing question about modern technology.

Can an artificial intelligence ever experience fear?

In the human sense, no.

An AI system has no racing heart, tightening chest, childhood grief, or instinct for survival. It does not lie awake anticipating loss. It does not feel dread when uncertainty appears.

Yet engineers build machines that perform some of fear’s functional work.

A system may detect anomalous data, reduce confidence, reject an input, trigger a warning, or move into a safer operating mode. A cybersecurity system may isolate suspicious activity. An autonomous machine may stop when its sensors become unreliable. A language model may refuse certain requests or signal uncertainty.

These behaviours can resemble alarm responses, but resemblance is not experience.

The machine does not panic.

It processes conditions specified by its architecture, training, and surrounding safeguards.

I think of this metaphorically as _Static Reset_: a system responding to instability by limiting, isolating, or discarding the state that produced it.

But even this metaphor has limits.

An unfamiliar input does not always make a model uncertain. Some models can respond to unfamiliar information with misplaced confidence. A model also does not ordinarily rewrite its learned parameters during a single conversation. Its internal activity changes as it processes input, but its deeper training remains largely fixed unless it is deliberately updated.

What looks like machine composure is therefore not courage.

It is the absence of feeling.

## Machines Reset, Humans Reinterpret

The deepest difference between human beings and machines is not that machines handle fear better.

It is that machines do not have fear to handle.

A system can discard temporary context because that context has no emotional meaning to it. It has no uncle inside the memory, no childhood bedroom, no smell of coconut oil, no desperate belief that one different action might have prevented death.

Human memory is more difficult to clear because it is woven into identity.

We do not merely store events.

We carry relationships, guilt, tenderness, shame, longing, and unfinished explanations.

This makes us vulnerable to false alarms. We continue obeying rules written by younger versions of ourselves, even after we have forgotten why those rules were created.

But human memory is not stone.

The same mind that once connected a hairbrush to death can begin to form another association: that the brush was never an omen, that coincidence is not responsibility, and that love does not make us the cause of every loss we endure.

A machine may reset.

A human being must reinterpret.

Perhaps healing begins when we stop asking whether the alarm feels real and begin asking who built it, what it was trying to protect, and whether the danger is still there.
