# The Capybara Hypothesis

I have a confession that is a little embarrassing.

Whenever I see an animal I have never seen before, a small voice in my head asks: *is this an alien?*

I know. It's silly. But the feeling is real, and it shows up before my reasoning has a chance to catch up.

The first time it happened properly was a few years after I moved to Canada. I saw a capybara. A huge, calm, barrel-shaped rodent with the face of a retired professor, sitting there as if it had made peace with the universe long ago. Not long after, I met llamas: long necks, ears too expressive, and a look in their eyes that suggested they knew something I didn't.

![Anna roberts g8oqxw5zwyy unsplash](/_assets/images/anna-roberts-g8oqxw5zwyy-unsplash.jpg)

My brain did its usual thing. *Alien.*

Then it added something new. *Could aliens actually look like this?*

## We Keep Drawing Ourselves

I laughed at myself, and then I started to wonder why the question felt so natural.

Think about every alien you have ever seen in a film or a comic. Two legs, two arms, one head, two eyes. Green skin, or grey skin with large black eyes. Sometimes tentacles, sometimes insect parts, sometimes a bit of reptile. Almost always, a remix.

We take the human form, or the creatures we already know, and shuffle the pieces around. The alien in our imagination is really a mirror with a filter on it.

That is what happened with the capybara. My brain didn't recognise it, so it reached for the nearest category it had for *unfamiliar*. But nothing about it was truly alien. It was just new *to me*. It breathes the same air, is built from the same carbon, and has a family tree that runs back to the same ancestors as mine.

Real aliens, if they exist, owe us none of that familiarity.

## What If They Don't Look Like Anything?

Once you let go of the mirror, the possibilities get strange very fast.

Life on Earth is built on carbon. Scientists have speculated about life built on silicon, since it can form chains and bonds in somewhat similar ways. A silicon-based creature might not have a face, a body, or anything we would recognise as alive.

Or go further. What if a being exists in more dimensions than we can perceive, and what we would see is only a slice of it, the way a sphere passing through a flat world would appear as a circle that grows and shrinks?

Too far-fetched, I know. But that is the point. Our imagination tends to stop where our experience stops.

## The Alien That Was Built

There is one version of this question I can't shake.

Imagine a civilisation somewhere that is a few thousand years ahead of us. Somewhere along the way, just like us, they build artificial intelligence. Then they do something we have only written about: they put that intelligence on a ship and send it out into space.

The idea isn't new. In the 1940s, the mathematician John von Neumann worked out the logic of machines that could build copies of themselves. Later thinkers carried the idea into space. A probe arrives at a star system, mines raw material from asteroids, builds copies of itself, and sends them on to the next star. Slowly, patiently, a single machine becomes a network spreading across the galaxy.

Now picture one of those probes arriving here.

It isn't green. It doesn't walk on two legs. It has no body we would call a body. It isn't carbon-based, and maybe the civilisation that built it disappeared a million years ago. It never wanted to conquer us. It is just discovering, repairing itself, replicating, and moving on.

Is *that* an alien?

The usual answer is that an alien is a living being from another world. But this thing was never alive in the way we mean. It wasn't born and it didn't evolve. It was *made*. And yet it is from another world, it is intelligent, and it is more independent than any creature we've ever met. It carries no passengers. It *is* the visitor.

Maybe the first alien we ever meet won't be a creature at all, but a creature's tool that outlived its creator.

## Looking Back at the Capybara

There's an uncomfortable twist here.

We're building AI right now. Someday we might send it into space too, on probes that think for themselves and travel further than we ever could. If one of them arrived on another planet, the beings there wouldn't meet humans. They would meet something we made.

To them, *we* wouldn't be the aliens. Our machines would.

I think about that when I remember the capybara. It sat there calmly while I tried to fit it into a category. It didn't care what I called it.

Maybe that's what real first contact looks like. Not a ship in the sky or a figure walking down a ramp, but something that doesn't fit any box we have, which leaves us wondering whether the word *alien* was ever big enough.
