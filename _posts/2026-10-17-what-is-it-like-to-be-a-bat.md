# What Is It Like to Be a Bat?

The bat came in through the window one summer evening at my grandparents' house, in that blue half hour after the lights go on and before anyone thinks to close the shutters.

I was nine, maybe ten. One moment the room was full of the smell of dinner and the clatter of steel plates. The next, something dark and soundless was looping just under the ceiling, tilting, dropping, banking an inch from the walls. My cousins screamed. An uncle grabbed a broom. Someone shouted to switch off the fan before it got hit.

My grandmother did not reach for anything. She turned off the lights, opened the far window wide, and told all of us to sit down and be quiet.

We sat in the dark, hearts pounding, listening to nothing. Then a faint flutter of air near my face, and it was gone. Out into the night it had come from, through a gap I could no longer even see.

I remember asking her how it found the window in the dark. She said bats don't need light. They see with their voices.

I did not understand what that meant. I spent the rest of the summer trying to imagine it, lying on the cool floor with my eyes shut, clicking my tongue at the walls.

I could not get there. All these years later, I still can't. It turns out a philosopher wrote a whole paper about why.

## Something It Is Like

In 1974, Thomas Nagel published a short essay in _The Philosophical Review_ with a title that sounds like a riddle a child would ask: _What Is It Like to Be a Bat?_

His argument begins with a definition so simple it is easy to miss how much it carries. An organism is conscious, he wrote, "if and only if there is something that it is like to be that organism, something it is like _for_ the organism."

Then he picks the bat on purpose. Bats are mammals, close enough to us that nobody doubts they experience something. But most of them perceive the world through echolocation, sending out "rapid, subtly modulated, high-frequency shrieks" and reading the echoes to judge distance, size, shape, motion and texture. It is clearly a form of perception. And, Nagel says, there is no reason to suppose it is "subjectively like anything we can experience or imagine."

Here is the part that took me straight back to that floor in my grandparents' house. Imagine, he says, that you have webbing on your arms, that you fly at dusk catching insects in your mouth, that you hang upside down in an attic all day and find your way by sound. You can do this, a little. But it only tells you "what it would be like for _me_ to behave as a bat behaves. But that is not the question. I want to know what it is like for a bat to be a bat."

That was exactly my failure as a boy. Clicking my tongue at the walls with my eyes shut, I was not becoming a bat. I was a nine-year-old pretending, with a nine-year-old's ears and a nine-year-old's brain, and the pretending only ever showed me myself.

Nagel's real target was the scientific confidence of his time: the belief that once we described the brain well enough, the mind would simply fall out of the description. He was not saying physicalism is false. He was saying we have no idea yet how it could be true. You can describe lightning completely without ever having seen a flash, because lightning exists outside the person watching it. But "what would be left of what it was like to be a bat if one removed the viewpoint of the bat?"

Experience is not something you look _at_. It is the looking.

## The Boy I Can No Longer Visit

I have written a lot on this blog about memory: how it reconstructs instead of records, how a convincing photograph can overwrite a real afternoon, how forgetting can be a kind of mercy. Reading Nagel, I realised there is a quieter problem underneath all of that.

I remember the bat. I remember the dark room, my grandmother's voice, the brush of air near my face. I can tell you those facts with total confidence.

But I cannot tell you what it was like to be that boy.

The fear in that room belonged to a body that was smaller, to a world where a bat could plausibly be a ghost, to a mind that did not yet know the word "echolocation." When I recall the scene now, I am not inside it. I am looking at it from outside, with an adult's vocabulary and an adult's calm, narrating a child's evening the way a scientist might describe an animal.

Nagel notices this too. The gap is not confined to exotic creatures, he writes. It exists between one person and another. And even in our own case, we would have trouble understanding our experience properly if we approached it from another point of view.

Memory, it turns out, is a lot like imagining the webbing on your arms. It tells me what it would be like for _me, now_, to stand in that room. It cannot hand me back the point of view of the person who was actually there.

In a way, every one of us is a bat to our former selves.

## The Machine That Describes Sonar Beautifully

A few days after reading the paper, I tried an experiment. I asked a language model to describe what it is like to perceive the world through echolocation.

The answer was lovely. It talked about the room arriving as a texture of returning sound, about soft surfaces swallowing the call and hard ones throwing it back bright and sharp, about a moth registering as a tiny flicker of shifting pitch. It was better written than anything I could have produced at nine, or honestly, today.

I have felt this before. I once wrote a whole post wondering why a machine could describe my own memory better than I could. But Nagel gives me a sharper way to name what is going on.

In a footnote, he points out that the phrase "what it is like" is misleading. It does not mean "what (in our experience) it resembles," but rather "how it is for the subject himself."

The model gave me resemblance. A fluent, beautiful stack of analogies built from human words about human senses. Nagel dismisses exactly this kind of thing near the end of the paper: loose comparisons like "red is like the sound of a trumpet" are "of little use," as anyone who has both heard a trumpet and seen red can tell you.

So the machine had done what I did on the floor. It described what it would be like for a human to behave as a bat. Only with a far bigger vocabulary.

## Standing Where the Martians Stand

Then the question turns around, and this is the part I cannot stop thinking about.

Nagel imagines intelligent bats or Martians trying to understand what it is like to be us. They might map the human brain in more detail than we ever will, and still have no idea what it is like to taste rain or miss someone. We would know they were wrong if they concluded there was nothing there, because we know what it is like to be us.

With AI, we are the Martians now.

We can open a model and inspect its weights. We can trace activations, measure attention, map circuits. We are learning about these systems in ways we could never learn about a bat's brain. And yet Nagel warned that this kind of objective study might take us further from the inner character of a mind, not closer. As he put it, even if you looked inside someone's brain and found a tiny image of the Mona Lisa there, you would have no reason to identify it with their experience of seeing it.

Nagel even saw the shape of this question coming. Functional descriptions of the mind, he wrote, could apply "to robots or automata that behaved like people though they experienced nothing." Then, in a footnote, he admits that perhaps anything complex enough to behave like a person would have experiences. But you could not find that out simply by analysing the concept.

I don't know if there is something it is like to be a language model. I suspect nobody does yet, whatever they claim. What Nagel taught me is to be suspicious of both easy answers: the confident "of course not, it's just maths," and the equally confident "listen to how it talks about itself, it must feel something." Fluency is not a window. A good description of sonar is not sonar.

## Clicking in the Dark

There is a hopeful footnote in the paper that I keep returning to.

Nagel mentions that some blind people find their way using a form of sonar, with vocal clicks or the taps of a cane. Perhaps, he writes, if you knew what that was like, you could roughly imagine the much more refined sonar of a bat. The distance between ourselves and other minds is not a wall. It falls somewhere on a continuum. "The imagination is remarkably flexible."

Maybe my tongue clicks on that floor were not entirely useless after all.

He closes the paper with a proposal: an "objective phenomenology," a way of describing experience that does not depend on empathy or imagination, that could at least partly explain to someone what it is like to have a sense they lack. He admits it would eventually hit a blank wall. But it might let us say much more, and much more precisely, than we can now.

As someone who builds tools for remembering, I find that both humbling and useful. My work so far has been about capturing what happened: the scene, the sound, the place. Nagel makes me wonder what a memory tool would look like if it tried to preserve the point of view as well. Not just the room, but how big the room felt. Not just the bat, but the fact that, to a nine-year-old, it might have been a ghost.

I don't know how to build that yet. Maybe nobody can, fully. But it feels like the right blank wall to walk towards.

## Turning Off the Lights

I think about my grandmother more than about Nagel when I remember that evening.

Everyone else in the room was trying to deal with the bat on human terms. More light. A broom. Louder voices. She was the only one who seemed to understand that the bat was living in a different world from ours, and that the kindest thing we could do was stop forcing ours onto it. So she turned off the lights, opened a window, and waited.

I suspect that is the most honest posture we can take towards any mind we cannot enter: a bat, a stranger, the child we once were, perhaps one day a machine. Not pretending to understand it. Not denying there is anything there to understand.

Just turning down our own way of seeing long enough to let it find its way.

I still don't know what it is like to be a bat.

But I know that there is something it is like. And some nights, that is enough to make me sit quietly in the dark.
