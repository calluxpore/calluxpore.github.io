# The Invasion

When I was a boy, the first monsoon rains always brought the ants.

They never arrived all at once. One afternoon there would be a single black thread running along the whitewashed wall of my grandmother's kitchen, so thin you could mistake it for a crack in the plaster. Nobody paid it any attention. Over a day or two the thread thickened, branched behind the steel almirah, and slipped quietly under the lid of the tin where she kept jaggery for our evening tea.

![Ants invasion](/_assets/images/ants-invasion.jpg)

We only noticed when it was already over. I remember prying the lid open and finding the dark brown block alive, every surface shimmering with tiny bodies that had moved in while we were busy with our lives. There had been no battle. No alarm. No single moment when the kitchen changed hands.

That was my first lesson in what an invasion actually looks like. It does not knock. It follows the paths you have already left open.

## The Invasion We Were Promised

The invasions I watched on television looked nothing like that. They came from the sky. Enormous ships slid over city skylines, casting shadows across entire neighborhoods, and the whole planet knew at once that something had arrived. *Independence Day*, *The War of the Worlds*, the silent shells hovering above the fields in *Arrival*. The enemy was always external, always visible, always unmistakably other.

Even our most famous invasion panic was mostly a trick of perception: the mass hysteria after Orson Welles's 1938 *War of the Worlds* broadcast was largely invented by the next morning's newspapers. The Martians never landed, but the story of people fleeing them did.

We spent a century rehearsing for an enemy we could point at. We built a cultural immune system tuned to ships, shadows, and sirens. It is entirely unprepared for something that arrives as a convenience.

## No Ships, No Shadows

Artificial intelligence did not descend from the sky. It arrived as autocomplete.

It came as a camera that quietly merges a dozen frames into the photograph you think you took. As a night mode that lifts shadows your own eyes could not see. As a phone that sharpens the moon with detail the lens never captured, a feed that decides which of your friends' lives you get to witness, a voice on a support line that pauses and laughs exactly like a person. None of it announced itself. Each arrival was a small improvement, and each improvement was welcome.

What makes this an invasion rather than a toolkit is where it settles. It does not occupy territory. It occupies the narrow space between the world and our senses.

Perception was never a clear window to begin with. The brain is a prediction engine. It guesses what is out there, then uses the thin stream of incoming signals to correct those guesses. What we experience as seeing is a controlled hallucination, kept honest by the stubborn friction of reality.

Now there is a second prediction engine standing in front of the first one.

```
Before:  World ──> [ Senses ] ──> [ Brain's Prediction ] ──> Experience

Now:     World ──> [ Model's Prediction ] ──> [ Senses ] ──> [ Brain's Prediction ] ──> Experience
```

When the signal reaching our eyes has already been predicted, smoothed, and optimized by a machine, the brain's hallucination is no longer being corrected by the world. It is being corrected by another hallucination. Two guessing systems, stacked one on top of the other, each assuming the other is the ground truth.

## Colonizing the Baseline

In 1995, the marine biologist Daniel Pauly gave a name to something I keep returning to: shifting baseline syndrome. Each generation of fisheries scientists accepted the fish stocks they saw at the start of their careers as normal, then measured every later decline against that already depleted starting point. Nobody remembered the abundance, so nobody could miss it.

Every successful invasion ends this way. The occupiers stop feeling like occupiers. They become the landscape.

I notice it in small ways. A raw photograph, before the processing kicks in, now looks faintly wrong to me: flat, grainy, unfinished. A sentence I type without suggestions feels clumsier than it should. Skin with pores, skies that are grey rather than blue, a voice that stumbles over a word. These used to be what reality looked like. Increasingly, they read as defects.

Then the invasion begins to run in reverse. Once synthetic perfection becomes the baseline, the real has to prove itself. A genuine photograph of a flood, an authentic recording of a politician, an honest handwritten note: each now arrives under a small cloud of suspicion. *Is this AI?* We are slowly losing the ability to spot the synthetic, and at the same time losing the reflex to trust the authentic.

The ants never needed to destroy the kitchen. They only needed us to stop seeing the thread on the wall.

## The Moat Around the Jaggery Tin

My grandmother never fought the ants with poison. Every monsoon, she filled a shallow steel plate with water and set the jaggery tin in the middle of it, a tiny island inside a moat. The ants would march to the edge, hesitate, and turn back. She never tried to rid the house of them. She simply decided where they were not allowed to go, and then she kept looking.

I think about that plate of water often now. Refusing AI outright is neither possible nor, honestly, desirable. Many of these arrivals genuinely help us see further than we could alone. But there is a difference between a tool we pick up and a lens we forget we are wearing.

The defense is not resistance. It is attention. Knowing which part of a picture came from the light and which part came from the model. Occasionally looking at the moon with nothing but your own eyes: blurry, pale, and true. Keeping a small island of unprocessed perception in the middle of everything, and checking it every monsoon for the thread on the wall.

The invasion did not come from the sky.

It came through our eyes, one helpful prediction at a time.

No one surrendered. We simply stopped noticing where the world ended and the model began.
